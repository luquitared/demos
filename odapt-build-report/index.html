What People Built on ODAPT

A deep qualitative + quantitative dive into the 630 apps, 706 generated builds, and ~580 build conversations in the production database · Companion to the database audit · Generated 2026-06-24

This report sets the headline metrics aside and asks one question: when you give 238 people an AI that turns plain English into working web apps, what do they actually make? The answer, read straight from their prompts and the HTML the agent generated, falls into a handful of strong patterns — and the two most revealing are exactly the ones worth dwelling on: people quietly building software for themselves, and people trying to launch a product.

1. The Shape of the Corpus

Of 630 apps, 180 (29%) are throwaway — "hello world", "test", "blah", default My Quick App shells, and /load session-restore stubs. That leaves ~469 apps with genuine intent, and those split cleanly into recognizable categories. Crucially, 37% of all builds produced 5+ distinct pages and many generated real project scaffolding (service-worker, manifest.json, package.json, README.md) — these were not all toys.

2. Theme One — People Building Software for Themselves

This is the most human pattern in the dataset, and the most commercially interesting. A meaningful slice of users weren't trying to launch anything — they had a personal or small-business problem and a spreadsheet, and they wanted a tool. The tell is the possessive: my data, my trading, my company, my staff.

"Do a dashboard for my data" — the bring-your-own-data reflex

The single most repeated internal-tool request was some variant of uploading a spreadsheet and getting a dashboard back. It recurs almost verbatim across many different users:

"I will upload an excel spreadsheet. Do a dashboard from its data"
"help me to upload an excel spreadsheet. After uploaded, do a dashboard of its data"
"I would like … a web application that imports CRM data via a csv file, that analyses this data and shows the most important [metrics]"
"I just uploaded sales data" · "i will upload csv files" · "do a dashboard for my data in excel"

16+ prompts reference uploading their own Excel/CSV/sales/CRM data; 26 apps generated a dedicated dashboard view. This is people using ODAPT as a personal BI tool — the "I have data, make it useful" job. One user even pointed it at a specific design system (berrydashboard.io) and asked it to replicate the layout with filters and CSV download.

Personal trackers & one-person business tools

These are unmistakably built for the author's own use, not for a market:

  • Personal trading journal — "Build me an app where I can track my daily p/l from my trading. It should also track deposits and withdrawals from each of my accounts."
  • Marda Jewelers Inventory — a named, real small business managing stock
  • MiS for commodity trading, purchase order finance application, deal management system
  • Expense report for staff (built twice — iterating), IT Helpdesk, HR development & training site
  • Teams monitoring & managing platform, Revenue management calculator for short[-term rentals]
  • M-Pesa PAYBILL directory (a Kenyan utility), "A dashboard of my company"
  • Habit trackers, pedometers, daily-task (Eisenhower-matrix) apps, voice-note alarms, photo-cataloguing systems
The insight. The "build for myself" users describe a concrete, often boring, real-world workflow — inventory, P/L, staff expenses, a data file that needs a chart. They convert at higher intent because they already know exactly what they want. This is the internal-tools / personal-software wedge (the Retool / Airtable / "spreadsheet superpowers" job), and it showed up organically without any prompting from the product.

Spec-driven founder prompts

These users wrote the way a PM writes a ticket — scope, core features, user flows. Examples pulled verbatim:

"Develop a mobile app to manage social media bookmarks. Users can sign up/login via email, Google, or Apple ID, with a password reset feature. The homepage displays recent bookmarks with title, description, tags, and preview image. A category page lets users sort bookmarks by topics…"
"Create an app for the 2025 NCAA men's March Madness tournament … 8 players each draft 8 teams … points based on the team's seeding … In the next round of the tournament…" (a full fantasy-draft scoring engine, ~829 turns)
"make an app that allows players to play The Price Is Right … An administrator can list up to 30 items … Players get to play in return for entering their email address." (a lead-gen game with an admin panel)

Several reached for real product infrastructure: 6 apps built genuine login + signup flows, apps generated register/auth/verify/admin/settings/profile views, and a handful produced PWA scaffolding (manifest + service-worker) so the result could install on a phone. One user explicitly wanted to ship it: "I will most likely add this to my GitHub page and also deploy it on the [association] hosting service… how could I handle the server-side email through GoDaddy hosting?"

The accidental "business consultant" sub-pattern

A cluster of Chinese-speaking users (12 prompts) didn't want an app at all — they used the AI as a startup strategist, and the agent dutifully rendered their plans as a business_plan view. Real examples (translated): a detailed plan for a BBQ + craft-beer + branded-sauce + livestream business ("I handle marketing, my friend is head chef… start as a street stall, then a storefront, then my own brand, then financial freedom and early retirement — plan it for me in meticulous detail"); a coconut-selling startup "from 0 to 1, as detailed as possible"; and a Xiaohongshu fruit content-commerce plan selling lychee, longan, jackfruit and mango from their hometown.

4. Theme Three — Games & Playful Builds

~24 game apps, skewed toward recognizable clones — the fastest way to test "can this thing actually build something interactive?" geoguessr (built repeatedly), Tetris, a Mario game "with mobile controls", memory games, a detective game, a 2D platformer, tic-tac-toe (9×9 variant), a Pokémon card generator, a frisbee-throwing game, nyan cat, and four separate "tinder swiper" builds. Games over-index on public visibility (nearly all public) — people built them to show off.

6. The Internal Tooling — ODAPT's Own Plugin Layer

Apps gained superpowers by attaching tools — a manifest/OpenAPI plugin system. Fifteen tools were registered, but the catalog was effectively team-built, not a user marketplace: the admin account (admin@odapt.ai) owns the core set, with a few built by early team members (madhav, lucasdawson23, aman). Five tools saw real user adoption:

ToolApps usingWhat it does (from its own instructions)
Firecrawl32"Use scrape and extract" — pull/structure web data
Postgres22Real DB; "Admin user has full permissions… app_user can only READ. Admin can configure RLS." Persistent app backends.
Replicate21"access and generate using any open source model" — image/AI generation by model URL
YouTube Captions17"Pull youtube transcripts!" — feeds summarizers/quiz makers
SQLite2Lightweight embedded DB

Read together, the tool mix reveals the platform's real ambition: not static sites, but data-backed apps — a generated frontend wired to a live Postgres database, a scraper, and a media-generation model. That's why the dashboard/data-upload and database-backed product requests landed; the plumbing existed. The team also seeded a clever image convention users picked up directly — magicimage.net/<prompt>.jpg for on-the-fly AI images and magicimagesearch.net/<query>.jpg for stock.

8. A Global, Multilingual User Base

For a six-week-old product, the linguistic spread is striking. Prompts appear in Chinese (12 — mostly the business-plan strategists), Spanish (data panels, finance school, "quiero que me hagas un…"), French (site vitrine business sites, an INNOVX company nav, interactive courses), Vietnamese, Indonesian ("bisakah kamu bantu saya buatkan…"), and Czech/Slovak ("potrebujem spravit dashboard…", "chci pomoci led postavit timer"). The product clearly leaked well beyond an English-speaking launch audience.

9. Patterns & Takeaways

  1. Two distinct intents, two distinct user types. "Build-for-myself" users arrive with a concrete workflow and their own data; "launch-a-product" users arrive with a brand name and a feature spec. They need different onboarding — the former wants data import + dashboards, the latter wants auth, a database, and a publish button.
  2. The internal-tools / personal-BI wedge appeared organically. "Upload my spreadsheet, give me a dashboard" was the most repeated serious request. With a real Postgres tool already in the stack, this was the most defensible, highest-intent job — and it wasn't being merchandised.
  3. Clones are the universal capability test. geoguessr, Tetris, Tinder, Zoopla, Netflix, Price Is Right — people probe a new builder by asking for something they already understand. Nailing a few showcase clones disproportionately drives confidence (and public sharing).
  4. The depth was real when intent was real. 37% of builds were 5+ pages; the top projects sustained hundreds of conversational turns and produced auth flows, PWAs, and project scaffolding. The engine could carry a serious build — the constraint was retention and publishing, not capability.
  5. The "AI as consultant," not app, is a latent product. A whole cohort used it to write business plans and strategy, not software. The agent happily obliged, rendering plans as pages — an unplanned use case that hints at an adjacent market.
  6. Global demand, zero localization effort. Six languages organically, with no evidence of i18n — suggesting the prompt-to-app value prop translates (literally) with almost no friction.
Bottom line. ODAPT's users sorted themselves into builders-for-themselves (internal tools, dashboards, personal trackers) and would-be founders (branded, spec'd products with auth and databases). The richest, most underserved seam was the first group's recurring ask — "here's my data, make me a tool" — which the platform was technically equipped to own but never productized.

Appendix — Method

Analysis ran against the full backup restored into a local PostgreSQL instance. App categories were derived by case-insensitive pattern matching over 630 app names (each app's opening prompt) plus full prompt text extracted from ~580 sessions message arrays; counts are approximate and marked with ~ where pattern-based. App structure (page counts, view names, scaffolding) was computed from the build.app_spec→views JSON. All quoted prompts are verbatim from the data (lightly truncated for length). Read-only; no data modified.
